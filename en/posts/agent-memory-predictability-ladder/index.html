Can Human Thought Be Fully Classified by AI? — The Predictability Ladder of Agent Memory

10/24/2026 Arlen Agent Memory, User Modeling, Predictability, Personality Psychology, Personalization

Can Human Thought Be Fully Classified by AI? — The Predictability Ladder of Agent Memory

This is the fifth and final installment of the five-part blog series “The Shannon Limit of Civilizational Wisdom,” addressing: Can an Agent, using only basic human information, achieve a complete classification of the diversity of human thought and predict human behavior and memory?

Introduction: A Debate Without a Referee

Getting an Agent to remember users, predict users, and ultimately “understand” users—this is one of the hottest frontiers in AI engineering today. The belief underpinning it is straightforward: if large models can compress all of human civilization’s text into parameters, then compressing “a person” into a user profile seems merely a matter of data volume.

Around this belief, the debate has long polarized into two camps. The engineering optimists cite digital footprint research and claim that “enough data + a good enough model = a complete personality mirror”; the philosophical negationists invoke free will and uncomputability, claiming that human nature cannot be captured by algorithms. Curiously, after years of argument, both sides still lack three things: a stratified verdict on “predictability,” an honest calibration of the strength of the evidence, and a precise definition of the word “complete.”

This article’s claim is: the answer is “no”—but it is a “no” with a precise internal structure. An Agent cannot, on the basis of demographics, education, generational background, and other foundational information, combined with cognitive-level and life-experience data, achieve a complete classification of the diversity of human thought—much less predict an individual’s concrete behavior and memory content. Yet at the level of distributions and traits, the accuracy of similar predictions has already reached an unsettling practical level. The gap between “complete” and “practical” is not a matter of engineering progress; it is sealed off by four principled upper bounds. Let us first nail down the terminology, then examine the evidence.

Terminology. In this article, “complete classification” means establishing a closed, exhaustive, and stable taxonomy over all dimensions of an individual’s thought; “digital twin” means an Agent trained on an individual’s data that can simulate that individual’s answers; “validity” means the strength of correlation between a prediction and the true outcome, expressed by a correlation coefficient rrr or ρ\rhoρ, where r=1r=1r=1 is perfect prediction and r=0r=0r=0 is random level.

The Evidence: How Far Has Prediction Come

Let us first give the optimists their due: partial predictability is not conjecture—it is a repeatedly verified fact.

The baseline is the 2013 PNAS study by Kosinski, Stillwell, and Graepel (n=58,466): using only Facebook “likes,” the model inferred race with 95% accuracy, male sexual orientation with 88%, and political leaning with 85%. Sharper still is the 2015 follow-up (Youyou et al., PNAS, n=86,220): with about 10 likes, the model’s judgment of a person’s personality surpassed that of their colleagues; with 70, their friends; with 150, their family; with 300, their spouse.

But note the precise meaning of these numbers—what they show is that on targets like the Big Five personality traits—low-dimensional, stable, with large between-group variance—“basic information + behavioral footprints” can outperform human judges. This is not “understanding the person.” The Cambridge Analytica affair is precisely a disenchanting sample: it claimed to have manipulated elections with psychographic profiling, but its actual campaign effects were never independently verified; the best measurable result in the field is Matz et al. 2017 (PNAS, covering over 3.1 million people)—matching ad copy to personality lifted click-through rates by about 40% and conversion rates by about 50%. Real but moderate. The market priced it as mind-reading; academia treats it as a predictor with an rrr of a few tenths. That gap is itself a microcosm of the entire field.

LLMs pushed this line forward by a generation. Argyle et al. 2023 showed that GPT-3, via “silicon sampling” (having the model role-play respondents of specific demographic backgrounds), can reproduce the attitude distributions of subgroups in American survey data; Park et al. 2024 went further—Agents built after two-hour qualitative interviews with 1,052 real Americans achieved roughly 85% normalized agreement with those individuals’ retest answers on General Social Survey items two weeks later, while a control condition using only demographic information scored significantly lower. This set of experiments is half of the direct verdict on this article’s question: basic information helps but falls far short of sufficiency; once interview data rich in personal narrative is added, prediction of “attitude-expression behavior” can reach considerably high accuracy—yet the 85% still measures response distributions on structured questionnaires, not predictions of concrete life choices.

The other half of the verdict comes from half a century of meta-analysis in personality psychology: conscientiousness, the strongest of the Big Five, has a corrected validity for job performance of only about ρ≈0.22\rho \approx 0.22ρ≈0.22, explaining less than 5% of the variance; self–other rating agreement falls in the range r≈0.3–0.5r \approx 0.3\text{–}0.5r≈0.3–0.5. The Brunswik lens model (1952) long ago explained why: the “ecological validity” of observable cues with respect to inner traits is itself limited, and no amount of perfect cue utilization can break through the hard ceiling of the information contained in the cues. Translated: the explanatory power of basic information about a person is real, statistically robust—and far less than one.

Principled Upper Bounds: Why “Complete” Is Impossible

Empirics answer “how much can be done”; the following four arguments answer “why it cannot reach completeness”—any one of them, standing alone, suffices to defeat the overall proposition.

First, chaos caps individual trajectories. In systems with a positive Lyapunov exponent (commonly called chaotic systems—systems where tiny differences in initial conditions amplify exponentially over time), each order-of-magnitude increase in initial information extends the prediction horizon by only a constant. A life trajectory can be likened to such a system (to be candid: no Lyapunov measurement has ever been performed on “a life”—this is an analogy, not a measurement result): a chance encounter, an illness, a single book rewrites the following decades in an irreducible way. An Agent can turn “born in Zhengzhou in 1985, computer science degree from a Project 985 university” into a good estimate of an attitude distribution, but for point predictions like “will this person quit their job to start a company next year,” the incremental value of prior information decays exponentially with the prediction horizon. Distributional predictability and trajectory predictability are two different mathematical objects.

Second, reflexivity: prediction changes the predicted. Once an individual learns the model’s classification of them, their behavior shifts—predictions of social systems therefore have no fixed point. Popper used this to argue that precise long-term social prediction is logically impossible; Soros called it reflexivity. An Agent that tells a user “you are the risk-averse type” is already manufacturing a user who no longer belongs to the original distribution.

Third, tacit knowledge is not in any input. Polanyi’s proposition—“we can know more than we can tell”—points out that most of the information underpinning individual judgment is situated, embodied, and cannot be articulated. Basic information covers only the part that is “articulable and already articulated.” Park 2024 happens to offer quantitative corroboration: two hours of high-quality interviewing (currently the closest available means of externalizing tacit knowledge) sharply raised prediction accuracy, yet above 85% there remains a residual that cannot be eliminated by further questioning. Unless the Agent accompanies the user through what the user experiences, this layer is in principle unreachable.

Fourth, the taxonomy strikes back: “complete classification” is a self-dissolving goal. The “looping effect of human kinds,” proposed by Hacking in 1995, points out that people are unlike quarks—those being classified read the classification, internalize or rebel against the labels, and thereby invalidate it. The entire history of MBTI, astrology, and even the “Gen Z” label conforms to this law. The day a classification is published is the day diversity regroups. A taxonomy of thought diversity is at best a moving target requiring continual re-estimation; it cannot be a completable object.

Responding to the Opposition: Isn’t 85% “Complete” Enough?

Opponents may argue that Park 2024’s 85% agreement rate is already close to the upper bound of humans’ own test–retest reliability—a person re-answering the same questionnaire two weeks later will not necessarily agree with themselves 100%. If the standard of “completeness” exceeds human self-consistency, then it does not hold for humans themselves, and this “no” is nothing but a straw man.

This objection carries real weight, and the cross-validation acknowledges it: the final verdict is therefore strictly confined to passive static modeling—that is, the Agent only observes, does not intervene, and uses only the existing information base. If any one of three conditions changes, the conclusion loosens: (a) if an intervention loop is included (the Agent actively experiments, or publishes classifications that induce users to self-fulfill them), completeness is not ruled out in principle; (b) if the information base expands to genomes, round-the-clock behavioral logs, or even sensorimotor channels, “shared embodied history” is demoted from a principled upper bound to an engineering-schedule problem; (c) if the prediction target is redefined from point prediction to distribution-level probabilistic pricing (the actuarial model), the difficulties of the situational layer are partially bypassed. In other words, the “no” seals off the completeness of passive modeling, not a line that can never be crossed—but acknowledging this precisely requires us to be honest about “when we are doing passive modeling.”

The Four-Tier Ladder: Rewriting a Yes/No Question as a Matter of Degree

Combining the evidence with the upper bounds, “is it predictable” should be rewritten as a ladder:

  • L0, the distribution layer (group attitude and behavior distributions): highly predictable. Silicon sampling has reached the level where it can substitute for real human subjects in pilot experiments. Boundary: distributions drift systematically after policy shocks and technological discontinuities.
  • L1, the trait layer (stable individual dispositions): moderately predictable. Digital footprints + basic information can reach r≈0.3–0.5r \approx 0.3\text{–}0.5r≈0.3–0.5, exceeding most human judges, but typically explaining less than 25% of the variance. Boundary: prediction is worst for the “deviants”—the few individuals in the tail of the thought distribution, the very people who historically drive change—and they carry the greatest weight.
  • L2, the situated-behavior layer (concrete choices): low and capped. Neuroscience’s strongest result (Soon et al. 2008: predicting button presses 10 seconds in advance at 60% accuracy against a 50% baseline) agrees with the Agent literature: single choices are near-random; only after aggregation across situations do they enter L1 territory.
  • L3, the memory-content-and-meaning layer (episodic memory and its subjective significance): unreachable in principle. What an Agent can store and retrieve are “records of memories,” not “memories.”

The shape of the final verdict is therefore: the Agent stands between L0 and L1; engineering can push it further toward L2; L3 is a horizon, not a target.

Engineering Implications: Turning the Verdict into Architecture

This stratified verdict is not philosophical small talk—it directly yields three engineering disciplines.

Memory architecture must reserve a place for the “incompressible.” Mainstream agent memory schemes (vector retrieval + structured profiles) are essentially compressors, and therefore inherit all the risks of compressors: optimizing for average hit rate systematically discards low-frequency but critical tail memories. The countermeasures: retain a raw event layer without lossy summarization, and list tail retention rate as a metric on par with compression rate; use prediction error / surprisal as the write signal—what is worth remembering is not what recurs, but what violates the existing user model.

User modeling stores distributions, not points. At the L0/L1 layers, one should build Bayesian profiles (i.e., representing judgments about the user as probability distributions rather than labels, continuously updated with new evidence), with confidence decaying under a reflexivity discount; at L2, make only short-term predictions with the prediction horizon explicitly annotated; at L3, do not model—only record. The looping effect also yields a counterintuitive guideline: allow users to see their own profile and rebut it—this seems to lower accuracy, but is in fact the only mechanism that keeps the model valid over the long term; a sealed-off classifier rots invisibly. Moreover, the data threshold for psychographic profiling has fallen as low as 10 likes, so the principle of the minimally sufficient profile (collecting only the features required by the current task) should be the default configuration.

Personalization treats unpredictability as a requirement, not a defect. Two decades of filter-bubble lessons from recommender systems show that taking prediction accuracy as the sole objective pushes users onto a collapsing trajectory of thought diversity. A mature personalization system must build in “anti-prediction” components: an explicit exploration budget, first-class-citizen treatment for tail preferences, and a Goodhart firewall that adds diversity metrics to the objective function (Goodhart’s law: once a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure). The advanced form of personalization is not predicting the user more accurately, but knowing more accurately at which layer one should not predict.

After Stripping the Halo: What Actually Bears the Weight of This Argument

An honest audit requires disclosing the real load-bearing structure of this argument. Once the rhetoric of “phase transitions” and “limits” is removed, the core verdict truly rests on only three sets of empirical anchors: the effect size r≈0.3–0.5r \approx 0.3\text{–}0.5r≈0.3–0.5 of digital-footprint prediction (Kosinski 2013; Youyou 2015), the 85% agreement rate of interview-based digital twins (Park 2024), and personality’s ρ≈0.22\rho \approx 0.22ρ≈0.22 prediction of real-world outcomes (Barrick & Mount 1991). Among the four principled upper bounds, the chaos argument is an analogy, the tacit-knowledge argument is a transplant of Polanyi’s proposition (not independently proven), and the looping effect seals off only passive prediction. The overall credibility rating after cross-validation is A-: the empirical anchors are solid, but two residual risks must be made explicit—the 85% comes from a single study (n=1,052) whose external validity is unverified; and the entire quantitative literature is centered on English-language/Western samples, with unknown transferability to Chinese contexts.

Conclusion: The Precise Shape of a “No” and Its Boundaries

Can an Agent, using only basic information, achieve a complete classification of the diversity of human thought and predict human behavior and memory? No—but this no is a ladder, not a wall. The distribution and trait layers have been compressed to a level approaching human self-consistency; the concrete-behavior layer is capped by chaos and situational interaction; the memory-and-meaning layer is in principle private. And this negation applies strictly to passive static modeling: intervention loops, an expanded information base, and redefinition of the target are three paths none of which has been ruled out in principle—under those conditions, “completeness” is an engineering-schedule problem, not a theoretical ceiling.

The boundaries of applicability must be stated clearly: this article’s quantitative conclusions rest on English-centric literature; Park 2024 is a single-study result; “a life trajectory is a chaotic system” is an analogy, not a measurement. Within these boundaries, the judgment that can be retained is this: an Agent can be an ever-clearer mirror, but the completeness of the mirror does not equal the identity of the subject—and that residual above 85% is not an engineering defect, but the information-theoretic trace of what it is to be human.
