The Shannon Limit of Civilization: The Lower Bound LLMs Are Approaching Exists—but Can Never Be Computed

10/20/2026 Arlen Information Theory, LLM, Compression, Scaling Laws, Emergence

The Shannon Limit of Civilization: The Lower Bound LLMs Are Approaching Exists—but Can Never Be Computed

This is Part 1 of the 5-part blog series “The Shannon Limit of Civilizational Intelligence.” It addresses four questions: the existence of the limit, estimability versus computability; the power laws and phase transitions of scale and generalization; whether compression extracts correlation or causation; and a compression-based account of “emergent capabilities.”

Introduction: How a Philosophical Question Became an Engineering Problem

“Compression is intelligence” has gone from Ilya Sutskever’s slogan to industrial reality: models with hundreds of billions of parameters spend day and night predicting the next token in humanity’s entire written corpus, and cross-entropy loss keeps falling toward a seemingly irreducible floor. This puts an ancient philosophical question on the lab bench: does the entire written record of human civilization, treated as an information source, have an incompressible lower bound? But what truly lacks rigorous discussion is not this yes-or-no question—it is three harder ones: how the limit is defined, what law governs the approach to it, and whether what settles inside the model as it nears the limit is statistical residue or the causal machinery of civilization. This article’s claim has four layers: the limit exists and its magnitude is estimable, but it is not exactly computable; the approach is macroscopically a smooth power law and microscopically a series of discrete “structure-discovery phase transitions”; compression necessarily captures high-order symbolic correlations, but “necessarily reconstructing the causal graph” is false; and “emergent capabilities” can be quantitatively decomposed into three things that have been conflated.

First, some terminology, each with a one-line lay explanation. Entropy: on average, how many yes/no questions it takes to pin down a message. Entropy rate: after seeing all prior text, how much uncertainty remains in guessing the next symbol. Cross-entropy loss: the model’s average surprise; its gap above the entropy rate is exactly what the model wastes relative to “perfect prediction.” Kolmogorov complexity: the size of a piece of information’s most essential compressed package (the length of the shortest program that generates it).

1. The Limit Exists: Two Measurements Seventy Years Apart Converge on the Same Interval

First, the definitional problem. A single language is approximately stationary, but civilization as a generative process is not—language evolves as it is written. So the working definition must introduce a stationarizing assumption: treat written history as an asymptotically mean-stationary stochastic process; if the Cesàro limit of its entropy rate exists, that limit is the lossless compression floor (the coding theorem of Gray & Kieffer 1980 shows that such limits exist under fairly general conditions). But note: this is an assumption that makes the problem definable, not a proven property of civilization—the scope of this article’s conclusions ends at “written history.”

After definition comes estimation. In 1951 Shannon had subjects guess the next letter of English text letter by letter, inferring conditional entropy from the guess distribution, obtaining 0.6–1.3 bits per letter. This number deserves a pause: if the English alphabet were uniform random, each letter would carry about 4.76 bits, meaning English text is roughly 73%–87% redundant—less than a third of a page is genuinely “new”; the compression limit is set by the remaining 13%–27%. Cover & King (1978) calibrated the same estimate with a gambling method (a good gambler is a good compressor; asymptotic wealth growth rate is exactly dual to entropy rate), arriving at about 1.3 bits per letter. This “prediction = gambling = compression” paradigm is the prototype of the LLM pretraining objective. Machine-side evidence converges too: the Hutter Prize record moved from about 1.37 bits per character in 2006 to about 1.1–1.2 in 2019, monotonically decreasing with no sign of saturation, suggesting we are already near the human-level estimate interval.

A skeptic might argue: these are all merely upper bounds on the true entropy rate, differing from each other by a factor of two; and Zipf’s law shows word frequencies are power-law heavy-tailed, so rare words may make finite samples systematically underestimate the true entropy—on what grounds claim the limit “exists”? Reply: a monotonically decreasing sequence of upper bounds is the standard way to approach an unreachable object; as long as the Zipf exponent α > 1 (English empirically sits slightly above 1, right near the critical point), entropy remains finite and consistently estimable, though one must acknowledge the estimation variance is large; the hardest answer is theoretical—

2. The Limit Is Not Computable: Existence and Computability Must Be Attributed Separately

Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff’s universal induction give the ultimate answer: the optimal compressor of any finite sequence is equivalent to its simplest program in the Kolmogorov sense. This framework proves two things that must be stated separately: civilization’s Shannon limit exists as a mathematical object; and at the same time it is not exactly computable—by the halting problem, no algorithm can output the true entropy of arbitrary text.

This is not a pessimistic footnote but the hinge of the whole discussion: “approaching the Shannon limit” can only proceed engineering-wise, and one can forever only claim “upper-bound improvement,” never “arrival.” This directly constrains the strength of claims about “how much LLMs have learned”: what can be said is not “the model has reproduced civilization’s generative mechanism,” but “the gap between the model’s cross-entropy and the best compression record is shrinking.”

3. The Dynamics of the Approach: The Macroscopic Power Law Is the Diluted Average of Microscopic Phase Transitions

Scaling experiments made the limit visible on engineering curves for the first time. Kaplan et al. (2020) found loss follows clean power laws in scale; Hoffmann et al. (2022, Chinchilla) corrected the resource recipe with a three-parameter form:

L(N, D) = E + A / N^α + B / D^β

The key is not the recipe but the constant term E ≈ 1.69 nats/token (about 2.44 bits/token, roughly 0.6 bits per character at ~4 characters per token). E is the loss component that cannot be reduced no matter how large parameters and data grow—it is the empirical upper bound of the entropy rate of that corpus distribution, the indirect appearance of civilization’s Shannon limit inside scaling experiments (a fitted extrapolation constant, consistent with the limit’s existence, not a direct proof of it). It lands exactly at the lower edge of Shannon’s interval—two measurements seventy years apart, made by completely different methods, converge here; this is indirect evidence that “the limit really exists.”

But the smoothness of the power law hides microscopic violence. Power et al. (2022) observed grokking on modular arithmetic tasks: training accuracy reaches 100% within a few hundred steps (full memorization), while test accuracy sits at random level for thousands of steps before suddenly jumping to perfection. Nanda’s team (2023) reverse-engineered it: during the plateau the model internally assembles an algorithmic circuit based on Fourier bases; when the weight of the “general algorithm” exceeds that of the “memorized lookup table,” the generalizing solution takes over. Translated into compression language: weight decay acts as a code-length penalty, forcing the model from a “parameters-as-data” memorization solution to a “high description efficiency” algorithmic solution, and the phase transition point is the moment the two solutions’ minimum description lengths cross. At the same time one must face the evidence of Zhang et al. (2017): sufficiently large networks can perfectly memorize random labels—this proves there is no automatic causal link between parameter count and generalization. Thus macro and micro are strung together by a single logic: natural language is a superposition of countless algorithmic substructures, and the macroscopic power law is precisely the statistical average of massive microscopic phase transitions diluted and stacked according to a Zipf-style frequency distribution.

A skeptic might argue: grokking has only been rigorously verified on small algorithmic tasks; extrapolating to natural language is metaphor, not evidence; Muennighoff et al. (2023) also showed that power laws need correction in the data-constrained regime. This objection partially stands—“natural language is composed of stacked microscopic phase transitions” is currently only a suggestive-level hypothesis; data constraint precisely raises E and pushes the limit farther away, without changing the phase-transition law itself. Boundary of conclusion: the phase-transition law is proof-grade on algorithmic subtasks and suggestive-grade on natural language globally.

4. Does Compression Yield Correlation or Causation? The Theorem Is Cold; the Evidence Is Layered

This is the most contested question. Information theory’s answer is cold: cross-entropy is minimized when the model distribution equals the true distribution, and the training objective is entirely agnostic to “which causal mechanism generated this distribution”—the same marginal distribution can be generated by infinitely many causal mechanisms. By Pearl’s ladder of causation (L1 association, L2 intervention, L3 counterfactual), pure distributional learning is in principle capped at L1; Bender & Koller’s (2020) “stochastic octopus” argument gives the same conclusion from the linguistic side: a symbol system without grounding makes no causal commitments. Therefore: “compression necessarily captures high-order symbolic correlations” is a theorem; “compression necessarily reconstructs the causal graph” is false, unless extra premises are introduced.

But controlled experiments show that the premises are sometimes indeed satisfied. Li et al.’s (2023) Othello-GPT was trained only on sequences of legal moves (pure symbols, no board, no rules); linear probes then found the model had spontaneously formed accurate internal representations of board state, and intervening on those representations caused subsequent moves to change accordingly in rule-consistent ways—this proves that at least on one class of tasks, a compression objective can reconstruct the latent state of the generative process, building an intervenable internal world model. The counter-evidence is equally rigorous: Jin et al.’s (2023–2024) corr2cause and CLADDER benchmarks show that on tasks requiring explicit do-calculus, LLMs perform near random, more like “reciting the statistical collocations of causal vocabulary.” Sutskever’s “compression is intelligence” argument is intuitively powerful, but its logical form is only “a good compressor is under pressure toward causality”—a motivation, not a theorem.

The reconciling position: human corpora are not purely observational data; natural language embeds massive interventional content (“because,” “causes,” “experiments show”); text is a noisy projection of the world’s causal processes, retaining traces of information beyond L1—this explains why models beat chance on some causal tasks and why they are unstable: what they inherit is the incomplete interventional information left in the projection, not intervention itself. The independent causal mechanisms principle further points out: if the world really is causally structured, a compressor approaching the Shannon limit will be forced to align internally with the causal structure (Richens & Everitt 2024 showed such alignment in controlled settings), but how deep the alignment goes remains open. Graded conclusions: capturing high-order correlation is proven; reconstructing latent state in controlled tasks is shown; distilling partial L2 ability from text is suggested; general L3 counterfactual ability is not ruled out, but the evidence is insufficient to assert it.

5. A Compression Account of Emergence: Deconstructing an Academic Standoff

Wei et al. (2022) documented “emergent abilities”: on many tasks small models tie with random chance, and performance jumps sharply after crossing some scale threshold. Schaeffer et al. (2023) fired back: when the metric is nonlinear (e.g., credit only if the entire string is exactly right), even smoothly improving underlying capability produces a spurious phase transition in the metric—many emergences are measurement artifacts. This article’s position: both sides are right, and the compression framework can state precisely where each is right.

Suppose the corpus is a mixture of algorithmic-structure sub-distributions, the i-th occupying token proportion pᵢ with eliminable redundancy Δᵢ, and the model successively “discovers” these structures; then global loss evolves as:

L(t) = E + Σ_{i ∈ U(t)} pᵢ·Δᵢ + ε(t)

where U(t) is the set of structures not yet grokked. This decomposition explains three phenomena at once: a single structure’s contribution to total loss is diluted by pᵢ, and thousands of small steps stack into a macroscopically smooth power law; but the accuracy of task i depends entirely on that one structure with tiny pᵢ—before grokking it is random, after grokking near perfect—the discreteness of task metrics comes from the discreteness of structure-discovery events; this is real emergence; while exact-match metrics completely hide partial progress during assembly, artificially sharpening the transition—this is the distortion layer Schaeffer measured. “Emergence” causes confusion because different papers alternately use it to name these three things. Olsson et al.’s (2022, Anthropic) observation of induction heads provides a mechanism-level anchor: within a narrow training window the model abruptly assembles an attention circuit of the form “[A][B]……[A] → predict [B],” in-context learning ability steps up in sync, and aligns with the kink in the loss curve—not “the curve bent,” but being able to point at that set of weights and say the qualitative change happened here, at this moment.

As for “whether there is a universal compression-ratio threshold for emergence,” the honest answer is: there is no universal constant, but there is a universal mechanism—generalization is the out-of-domain extension of compression, and emergence is the moment some structure’s compression extends from in-domain to out-of-domain. A skeptic might argue: without a universal threshold this is not a quantitative theory, only post-hoc narration. Partially accepted—the framework currently provides mechanism and decomposition, but its predictive power is not zero: it predicts kinks in the loss curve (verified by induction heads), S-shaped curves under continuous metrics (verified by the mirage paper), and synchronous emergence across multiple tasks sharing the same underlying structure (preliminary support)—three testable implications beyond post-hoc narration. Boundary of conclusion: quantitative at the “decomposition formula” level, not yet at the “universal threshold” level.

Conclusion: A Confidence Map, and a Question Leading to the Individual

The article’s assertions collapse by evidential strength: Proven (theorem-grade)—the limit is not exactly computable and can only be approached by a sequence of upper bounds; compression necessarily captures arbitrarily high-order conditional dependencies. Shown (strong empirical)—the limit exists and English’s magnitude is 0.6–1.3 bits per letter (Shannon, Cover & King, the Hutter Prize, and Chinchilla’s E term converge); controlled tasks can reconstruct latent state and support intervention (Othello-GPT); the mechanistic decomposition of emergence (three parties of evidence interlock). Suggested (hypothesis-grade)—the macroscopic power law is the diluted superposition of microscopic phase transitions; interventional traces in text can distill partial L2 causal ability. Not ruled out—general counterfactual causal ability; a universal compression-ratio threshold for emergence. The scope boundary must be stated plainly: all of the above is confined to within the text modality; the missing grounding of symbols in the physical world is an intrinsic property of text as an information source, not a defect that scaling text data can remove.

Finally, an interface to the next installment: if the conditional entropy of civilization’s text still leaves a residue of 0.6–1.3 bits per letter, then given a person’s demographic background, life history, and cognitive level, how much conditional entropy remains in their thoughts and behaviors? What an Agent’s memory capability needs to compress is exactly this residue—and it is not a simple subset of civilization’s limit, because an individual’s irreducible randomness may be far higher than the average redundancy of text suggests.
